# **SECTION E: Edge Cases and Conflict Scenarios**

*These help us handle situations where a student's profile does not clearly fit one degree.*

E1. If a student has strong Mathematics but their primary interest is in business rather than technology, which degree would you lean toward recommending, and why?

Your answer: Accounting, Actuarial Science, Finance, Data Science, Statistics and so on. Students who are strong in Mathematics can explore fields related to Mathematics, data analysis and logical reasoning. These students are generally more suited to careers that require calculation skills, analytical thinking and problem solving ability. 

E2. If a student loves science and also has strong empathy and social skills, how do you distinguish between recommending Medicine versus Psychology?

Your answer: It depends on whether the student is more interested in exploring scientific fields or understanding human personality and behavior. When a student chooses a career interest that suits them more, the other traits may simply become part of their working style or personality in the workplace. Therefore, there is no need to worry or overthink it too much.

E3. Are there any degree programs where the student's minimum grade or academic threshold is non-negotiable, meaning no matter how interested they are, you would not recommend it below a certain academic level?

Your answer: Of course. For example, science-related courses are generally more suitable for science stream students, as arts stream students may not have the academic foundation required to apply.

However, for certain technical engineering fields, arts stream students may still have the opportunity to enter the engineering field through technical courses at a Polytechnic before progressing further. Nevertheless, this pathway is not available for all engineering disciplines.

E4. Some students cite salary expectations as their primary reason for choosing a degree. How do you handle this in your counseling, and should a system like ours factor this in?

Your answer: Yes, many students have been thinking about this in recent years. I usually tell them that there is no right or wrong choice. However, their attitude decides how the employers judge them and how much salary can be offered. An employee with a poor work attitude may not even pass the probation period, so there would be no basis to discuss a high salary.

Fresh graduates should also avoid focusing too much on salary during the interview with employers. Have they considered what strengths, abilities, or value they can offer in exchange for a higher salary, especially when they have little or no working experience? In the end, whether a person can earn a higher salary in the future is often determined by their attitude and work ethic.

I do not encourage students in the education field to place salary expectations as their top priority. Education is a profession that requires a strong sense of mission, responsibility, and compassion. If someone chooses to become a teacher mainly because of the salary or because they think the job leaves extra time for side businesses and additional income, such individuals are less likely to devote their full commitment to education. Therefore, they may not be suitable to become educators.

E5. Is there any degree on our list that you feel is frequently misunderstood by students — where their expectation of the course does not match reality?

Your answer: Generally, it will be fine as long as students understand their own abilities and choose a course that they are interested in and capable of pursuing. 

However, I do have students who changed their courses midway after entering university because they became more aware of other fields. Still, the impact was usually minimal, and they managed to graduate successfully.

As for whether their future careers will definitely be related to the courses they studied, that is another matter.
